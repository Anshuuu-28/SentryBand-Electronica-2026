Real-Life Use of the Model

What SentryBand looks like in the moment it matters — sensing a fall, alerting a caregiver, without cloud or delay.

Concept visualization (AI-generated) illustrating the intended real-world use case — not footage of physical hardware, which is targeted for completion by Sep 14 (see "Honest limitations" in the README).

Healthcare can't watch everyone, all the time.

Chronic illness is rising while the ratio of caregivers to patients keeps shrinking. Most monitoring today is either too infrequent to catch an emergency, or depends on a cloud connection that fails exactly when it's needed most.

01

Clinic visits catch problems late

Checkups happen weeks apart. A fall or cardiac event can go unnoticed for hours.

02

Too few caregivers per patient

Continuous human supervision isn't possible at the scale chronic illness demands.

03

Cloud wearables fail silently

Devices that need constant internet miss alerts in dead zones — and ship health data off-device.

04

Single-signal tracking misses risk

Most fall detectors ignore heart rhythm; most fitness trackers ignore falls.

From raw sensor to alert, entirely on the wrist

Every stage — sensing, feature extraction, classification, and fusion — runs on the device itself. No round trip to a server.

Sense continuously

An accelerometer and a PPG heart-rate sensor take fresh readings every second, around the clock.

Classify on-chip

Two small int8-quantized neural networks — 7.56 KB combined — score each window for a possible fall and for an abnormal heart rhythm.

Fuse the two signals

A fusion rule combines both scores into one of four states: Normal, Possible Fall, Heart Alert, or Combined Emergency — the last reserved for when both signals fire together.

Alert instantly, locally

Buzzer and LED trigger on the wrist immediately. Only the state and a short summary — never raw sensor data — reach a paired phone.

where it still gets confused

Real Heart Alert windows are mistaken for Normal about 34% of the time, and Combined Emergency is now split almost evenly with Fall — both honest targets for the next iteration, and both got measurably harder once trained on all 15 real PPG-DaLiA subjects instead of 6. Fall detection remains the strongest signal, correctly caught 42 of 46 times.

Why 71.5%, not 95%. An earlier version of this model, trained on data we generated ourselves, scored above 90% — a common and misleading result, since synthetic signals are easier to classify than real ones. Once validated against real SisFall and PPG-DaLiA recordings, accuracy settled at a lower, truer number — and grew even more honest once trained on all 15 real PPG-DaLiA subjects instead of a smaller sample, which measurably increased task difficulty. We're reporting that fully-diverse number — and we went further: cross-checking the heart-alert model against real MIMIC PERform AF (genuine ICU atrial fibrillation) recordings, separately, surfaced a real cross-device generalization gap. That result isn't in the deployed model — see "Data sources" below.

Real ICU PPG recordings during confirmed atrial fibrillation. We tested training the heart-alert model directly on this real arrhythmia data instead of our single-device approach — it revealed a genuine cross-device domain gap (accuracy dropped, not improved), so it was not included in the final model.
